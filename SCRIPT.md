# AISLE 9 — full 9-minute script (6 parts × 90 s)

**Format:** vertical 1080×1920, 30 fps, 2,700 frames per part / 16,200 total.
**Voice:** one narrator — Mara, first person, past tense. Calm, observant, quietly frightened. ~3.5 words/s.
**Opening frame:** APPROVED — Mara stocking Aisle 9 at 11:59, empty store, rain-streaked glass automatic doors at the far end (`NightShiftShort`, frame 30).
**World rule:** an experimental emergency broadcast on the old radio tower freezes people in their last action, loops the building back to 11:59 at 12:07, and forces one command: *"Stay inside."*

---

## PART 1 — 12:07 (0:00–1:30)

**Visual hook (first 3 s):** the approved opening frame — Mara reaching up to stock a can, shelves on both sides, glass doors and rain behind her. The radio song. Then the intercom clicks.

**NARRATION (≈310 words):**

> At 11:59 on a Tuesday night, I was alone in Aisle 9, stacking cans of peaches that nobody was going to buy before dawn.
>
> The rain had been falling since six. It drummed on the skylights and turned the parking lot into a black mirror. The radio behind the register played the same soft song on a loop. The other stockers had left hours ago. I was nineteen, I had rent due Friday, and my mother's medication cost more than I made in a week. So I worked the closing shift, and I told myself I didn't mind.
>
> There's a legend in this town about the supermarket. Don't work the night shift alone, they say, because the intercom knows your name. I used to laugh at it. I laughed the night I took the job.
>
> At 12:07, the intercom clicked.
>
> "Mara," it said.
>
> I dropped a can. It rolled under the shelf and kept rolling, which was impossible, because the floor was level. The voice was calm and warm and patient. It said my name again.
>
> The automatic doors at the front slid open. Rain blew in across the entrance mat. A man walked through them in a soaked brown coat, water dripping from his hair onto the tile. He didn't have a cart. He didn't look at the registers. He walked straight down Aisle 9, past me, and stopped at the endcap.
>
> Then he folded to the floor like his strings had been cut.
>
> I ran to him. I'm trained in first aid; I've done it a hundred times in drills. I got down on my knees, checked his pulse, and reached for the defibrillator on the wall.
>
> That's when he stood up.
>
> He wasn't moving like a man who'd been unconscious. He moved like something being pulled. His head turned toward me too slowly. His smile was wrong — too wide, too still.
>
> And when he spoke, it wasn't his voice.
>
> It was my mother's.
>
> "Mara," she said, warm and patient and calm. "Don't go home."
>
> My mother had been dead for two years.

**Ending cliffhanger / final line:** *"My mother had been dead for two years."* — hard cut to black.

**Key shots:** opening aisle creep → intercom close-up → can rolling under shelf → glass doors sliding open → wet coat entering → fold to floor → defibrillator wall → slow head turn → mother's-voice close-up.

## PART 2 — DON'T GO HOME (1:30–3:00)

**Opens on the payoff:** Mara backs into the shelving; the man doesn't follow, only repeats the sentence.

**NARRATION (≈305 words):**

> I backed into the shelving until the metal bit into my spine. The man in the wet coat didn't follow. He just stood there with that smile, repeating the sentence the way a scratched record repeats a single bar.
>
> "Don't go home. Don't go home."
>
> I ran.
>
> I locked myself in the manager's office and held the door handle until my hands stopped shaking. Through the little glass window I could see the aisles, empty and bright. I could see the front doors.
>
> That's when I noticed the parking lot was wrong.
>
> The last car I'd seen pull out had been a silver sedan. Through the office window, it was pulling in. Then it pulled out. Then it pulled in again. The wipers moved backward. The rain fell upward for half a second and then fell down again.
>
> I thought I was losing my mind. I pulled up the security footage on the office monitor to prove I wasn't.
>
> There he was. The man in the brown coat, walking through the front doors. Twelve oh seven. I scrubbed forward. There he was again. Twelve oh seven. I counted forty-seven separate entries, every one at the exact same minute, every one with the same tilt of the head.
>
> Dave, the security guard, came in with coffee and asked why I was hiding. I showed him the monitor. He laughed — too loudly — and said the camera clock was broken. Then his walkie-talkie crackled.
>
> It always crackled before an announcement. I'd noticed that. I just hadn't understood what it meant.
>
> The PA chimed.
>
> "Closing time in five minutes," it said.
>
> The voice was mine.
>
> One by one, the lights went out. Aisle three. Aisle four. The freezers hummed on in the dark. On the monitor, the store was empty — no wet coat, no customer, no movement at all.
>
> So who was counting down? And why did the voice sound like me?

**Key shots:** backed-into-shelves → manager's office door → parking lot loop through glass → CCTV monitor grid with 47 timestamps → Dave's coffee and walkie crackle → lights dying aisle by aisle → empty monitor.

## PART 3 — THE LOOP (3:00–4:30)

**Opens on a hard reset:** Mara blinks; it's 11:59 again, same song, same rain.

**NARRATION (≈315 words):**

> I blinked, and the radio was playing the same song from the beginning.
>
> The clock on the register said 11:59.
>
> I was standing in Aisle 9 with a can of peaches in my hand, exactly where I'd been four minutes ago. The rain was falling the same way. The same silver sedan sat in the same parking space. Nothing had happened yet.
>
> Except that I remembered all of it.
>
> I put the can down very carefully. My hands were steady now. Fear will do that — it burns off the panic and leaves something cold behind.
>
> I had eight minutes. I used them.
>
> I didn't go near the endcap. I took the emergency flashlight from behind the counter and a bottle of water and the good boots from the employee locker. Then I found the service corridor behind the staff room — the one the store manager had shown me during orientation, the one that climbed to the roof.
>
> On my way I heard someone crying.
>
> Dave was in the hallway by the loading dock, facing the wet customer. Except it wasn't the wet customer anymore. It was a woman in a blue uniform, one of the deli staff who'd clocked out at nine. She stood with her arms at her sides, whispering. Dave was whispering too.
>
> I pulled him behind a pallet. He fought me for a second, then went limp.
>
> "They don't chase you," he said. His voice sounded far away. "They just... wait."
>
> I didn't ask what they were waiting for.
>
> Halfway down the freezer aisle I saw a flash of yellow between the ice cream doors. A kid. Small, maybe eight, crouched behind a stack of frozen pizzas with his knees pulled to his chest. He looked at me with enormous eyes and put one finger to his lips.
>
> I had no time. The clock said 12:04. I climbed.
>
> The roof was a landscape of rain and steel. The old radio tower rose above the store, and the antenna at its base was pulsing — a slow, steady heartbeat of blue light. I looked over the parapet.
>
> Below, in the parking lot, hundreds of people stood in perfect rows, facing the store. They weren't moving. They weren't attacking.
>
> They were whispering, all of them, in one voice.
>
> "Stay. Inside. Mara."
>
> Three words. One voice. And now I knew whose it was.
>
> Mine. In about six hours.

**Key shots:** register clock reset → can placed down → supply grab → service corridor → Dave and the blue-uniform listener → pallet rescue → freezer kid with finger to lips → rooftop antenna pulse → rows of whispering people below.

## PART 4 — THE LEGEND (4:30–6:00)

**Opens on the whisper repeating:** *"Stay. Inside. Mara."*

**NARRATION (≈310 words):**

> The whisper came through the roof hatch, up the ladder, into my bones.
>
> I went back down.
>
> The kid was still in the freezer. His name was Leo. He'd been hiding since the first night — since before I was born, he said, which couldn't be true, except that he knew things nobody could know. He knew about the price gun that clicked once every loop. He knew about the song on the radio. He knew my mother's name.
>
> "They're not sick," he said. "They're waiting for the command to finish."
>
> He told me the story the way a child tells a fairy tale, flat and certain. Thirty years ago, a night stocker heard the intercom call his name. He panicked and locked every door in the building. He kept the customers inside for a week. When the police finally broke in, everyone was alive. Everyone was calm. Everyone said the same three words.
>
> The town called it a miracle. Then they forgot it. Then they turned it into a warning: don't work the night shift alone.
>
> I found the file in a sealed maintenance cabinet under the stairwell. PROJECT LULLABY. Containment trial. Emergency broadcast system installed on the tower to keep people orderly during an evacuation. The field notes said the signal didn't make people violent. It made them stop. It froze them inside their last action and looped the building's time back to 11:59, again and again, waiting for someone to give the command a different ending.
>
> There was a list of test subjects at the back. Most of the names were crossed out.
>
> Mine wasn't. My mother's was — but not like the others. Hers had been scratched away so hard the paper had torn.
>
> I looked into the dark glass of the freezer door.
>
> My reflection was standing outside it. Not in the glass — outside, in the parking lot, with the rows of whispering people. My reflection turned its head and smiled with my mouth.
>
> "The loop isn't trapping me in the store," I said.
>
> My reflection kept smiling.
>
> "It's teaching me to become one of them."

**Key shots:** ladder descent → Leo in the freezer → 30-years-ago flashback montage (ink-outlined, desaturated) → PROJECT LULLABY file → scratched-out name → freezer glass reflection.

## PART 5 — THE CHOICE (6:00–7:30)

**Opens on the reflection:** she smashes the freezer glass; her reflection doesn't move.

**NARRATION (≈300 words):**

> I smashed the freezer glass with the butt of the flashlight.
>
> My reflection didn't move.
>
> I had four minutes. I had a kid who remembered too much, a security guard who wasn't answering his radio, and a building full of people waiting for a word.
>
> So I built a trap.
>
> We dragged shopping carts into the aisles and chained them with produce straps. We set the yellow price gun behind a tripwire of fishing line at the top of the service stairs — one click, and the PA would play a recorded countdown. We opened every freezer door and let the fog roll out across the floor, thick and white, so nobody could see us coming.
>
> Dave was at the register, staring at the walkie in his hand. "I didn't know," he said. But his voice had that same calm, patient quality. The walkie had been live the entire time. Every place I went, every door I opened, the signal had known.
>
> He wasn't betraying me. He wasn't even awake.
>
> I locked him in the walk-in with a bottle of water and a coat. It was the only way to keep him safe.
>
> The PA began the countdown.
>
> Ten. Nine. Eight.
>
> I climbed the service ladder with Leo's drawing in my jacket — a picture of his mother, drawn from memory, the only thing the loop couldn't take from him.
>
> At the top, beside the pulsing antenna, the maintenance panel had two switches.
>
> OFF. End the broadcast. Stop the loop. Nobody knew what was really outside the doors — and nobody would ever remember what happened inside.
>
> BROADCAST. Overwrite the message. Become the new voice. Keep the loop running, but change what it says.
>
> The clock in my head hit twelve oh seven.
>
> Off would stop the night. Broadcast would stop the voice.
>
> I had eight seconds to decide which one was me.

**Key shots:** glass shatter → cart barricades → price-gun tripwire → freezer fog → Dave's walkie → walk-in door closing → countdown overlay → antenna panel with OFF / BROADCAST.

## PART 6 — AISLE 9 (7:30–9:00)

**Opens on her hand on the switch:** she chooses BROADCAST.

**NARRATION (≈300 words):**

> I threw the switch to BROADCAST.
>
> Then I did the only thing the command couldn't parse.
>
> I didn't give an order. I gave a memory.
>
> I thought of my mother's laugh — the way it started in her throat before it reached her mouth, the way she covered her face when she was really laughing. And I said the thing she actually used to say, the thing I'd been waiting two years to hear again:
>
> "Go home. It's over."
>
> The whisper stopped.
>
> Not all at once. It moved through the rows like wind through a field, one person at a time, until the parking lot was silent. A woman in a blue uniform lowered her arms. A man in a wet coat looked down at his own hands and began to cry. Someone said a name. Then another. Then a hundred names, messy and overlapping and human.
>
> The rain stopped.
>
> Real dawn came through the glass doors, thin and gray and ordinary. I released Dave from the walk-in. He hugged me for a long time. Leo ran into his mother's arms — a woman who had stood in that parking lot for thirty years, waiting to be told she could go.
>
> I walked out through the front doors into a silent morning. The air smelled like wet asphalt. It was the first real thing I'd smelled in hours.
>
> Behind me, Aisle 9 was empty.
>
> The yellow price gun lay on the floor where I'd dropped it.
>
> And the intercom clicked once.
>
> My voice — the broadcast I'd left behind in the tower — said:
>
> "If you're hearing this, check aisle nine."
>
> The camera on the ceiling turned.
>
> In a store three towns over, a night stocker alone at 12:06 looked up from stacking cans as the automatic doors began to slide open.

**Final image:** the new night stocker at 12:06, doors sliding open — same composition as the approved opening frame, mirrored. Hard cut to black.
