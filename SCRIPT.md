# The Wave That Knocked a Train Off Its Tracks

## Facts and sources (researched; every joke below is a real fact)

- On January 15, 1919, a 50 ft (15 m) molasses tank at 529 Commercial Street, Boston, ruptured and released about 2.3 million US gallons (8,700 m³), roughly 13,000 short tons. The wave reached an estimated 35 mph (56 km/h), killed 21 people and injured about 150. Source: [City of Boston, "100 years ago today: Molasses crashes through Boston's North End"](https://www.boston.gov/news/100-years-ago-today-molasses-crashes-through-bostons-north-end); [Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Great Molasses Flood"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Great-Molasses-Flood).
- The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company (bought by USIA in 1917). Molasses was fermented into industrial alcohol for munitions during and after WWI. The man overseeing construction, Arthur Jell, had no engineering or architectural experience and skipped basic safety tests. Source: [Wikipedia, "Great Molasses Flood", Background and Causes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Molasses_Flood).
- The tank leaked from early on; local children collected pails of molasses at the seams, and the company painted the tank brown to hide the leaking syrup. Source: City of Boston, above; Wikipedia, Causes.
- The day before the collapse, a tanker delivered warm molasses to lower its viscosity. Temperatures rose from about 2 °F to 41 °F in the preceding days. Structural defects, thin low-manganese steel, possible fermentation gas, and the warm new load all contributed. A 2014 engineering analysis found the steel was about half as thick as it should have been. Sources: Wikipedia, Flood and Causes; City of Boston.
- At about 12:30 p.m. the failure started near a manhole cover at the base; the tank collapsed. The wave buckled the elevated railway and tipped a streetcar; it moved a firehouse off its foundations. Source: City of Boston; Wikipedia, Flood.
- Molasses is about 40% denser than water. It was shear-thinning under pressure and heat, then cooled and thickened quickly in the winter air, trapping victims. Source: [Scientific American, "The Science of the Great Molasses Flood"](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-science-of-the-great-molasses-flood/); Wikipedia, Flood.
- The owners claimed anarchists had bombed the tank. In 1925 a court-appointed auditor found the owners negligent; USIA ultimately paid $628,000 in damages. Source: Wikipedia, Aftermath; City of Boston.
- The disaster changed US construction rules: engineers had to sign and seal plans, and building inspectors had to examine projects. Source: City of Boston; Wikipedia, Aftermath.
- The tank was never rebuilt. Its concrete slab is still about 20 inches (51 cm) below the surface of the baseball diamond at Langone Park. Residents reported the smell of molasses on hot days for decades afterwards. Source: Wikipedia, Area today; [HISTORY, "Why the Great Molasses Flood Was So Deadly"](https://www.history.com/articles/great-molasses-flood-science).

## Script

Voice: one steady, deadpan tone. No captions, no labels. Target 55–70 s at about 3.7 words per second (about 248 words / 67 s).

Retention design: the hook opens a loop ("the reason is buried under a baseball field") that is only closed in the final two lines. The middle escalates in numbers (50 feet → 2.3 million gallons → 35 mph → 21 dead), then flips with the anarchist excuse, then pays off with the rule and the buried slab.

Thirteen thousand tons of molasses once knocked a train off its tracks. The reason is buried under a baseball field.

It was January 1919, in Boston's North End. The tank stood fifty feet tall, right above the elevated railway.

The company had no engineer. Molasses meant alcohol, and alcohol meant munitions. The war had just ended. Nobody tested the tank.

Locals knew it leaked. Kids filled pails from the seams. The owners painted the tank brown so the syrup wouldn't show.

On the fifteenth, Boston woke up freezing. By lunch it was forty degrees. A tanker had delivered warm molasses the day before.

Cold steel, warm syrup, a fresh load, and steel half as thick as it should have been.

At twelve thirty, the rivets let go. A manhole cover flew off. The tank folded, and two point three million gallons went sideways.

Luckily, it wasn't water. Unluckily, molasses is forty percent denser, and it moved at thirty-five miles an hour.

Think of a soda bottle left in a hot car. The drink doesn't get angrier; the pressure does. Only this bottle was fifty feet tall.

The wave buckled the railway, moved a firehouse, and killed twenty-one people. The owners blamed anarchists. The court blamed the tank.

After that, engineers had to sign and seal their plans, and inspectors had to check them. That rule came out of a syrup puddle.

The tank is gone. Its concrete floor is still there, twenty inches under a baseball diamond.

On hot days, the whole park smells like molasses.
