He was halfway through the last box of peaches when the store went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. The refrigerator hum, the radio low behind the register, the rain ticking on the skylights. Nineteen years old, alone on the closing shift, and a rent payment he couldn't make.

He finished the row, taped the carton shut, and walked across the store to restock the other side.

The automatic glass doors were on his way.

Through them, the parking lot was a black mirror. And something in it was moving.

A man, or what used to be a man, in a green coat, limping. He was pressed against a parked car, slamming one hand into the window. Again. And again. The same motion every time, like a machine that had forgotten how to stop.

The stocker set down the box.

He walked to the glass. Up close, the details were worse. Skin the colour of old leaves. A jaw hanging wrong. Eyes that didn't look at anything. Rain running off him in sheets, and he didn't seem to feel it.

The thing turned.

He ran for the shutter controls beside the door and hit the button. The steel shutter came down with a scream of metal, slow, too slow, grinding toward the floor while the thing outside turned toward the sound.

Six seconds. That's how long it takes a steel shutter to seal a supermarket. He counted every one of them.

When it finally hit the floor, the parking lot went dark and the store stayed bright. For the first time that night, he was safe.

Then the thing outside looked up at the camera.
